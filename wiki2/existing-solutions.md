# Existing Solutions in Southeast Asia

Programs and businesses already working on fast-fashion and textile waste in the region, set against the one government system in this wiki that has actually scaled to solve a comparable household-waste problem.

## Southeast Asia's own responses

- **Kloth Cares (Malaysia)**, founded **2016** under the theme "Keeping Fabrics Out of Landfills," had collected **over 18,000 kg of fabric by 2017**, with a **2019 collection goal of 188,888 kg**. Set against Malaysia's own estimated **~2,000 tonnes of textile waste a day**, that first-year collection figure is a rounding error on the scale of the problem — not a criticism of the program, just the size of the gap it's working against. [Source](../sources/sea-kloth-cares-circular-fashion.md)
- **Style Theory (Singapore)** is a clothing rental service: customers rent for an event and return it, substituting rental for buying-and-discarding. [Source](../sources/sea-kloth-cares-circular-fashion.md)
- **moreloop (Thailand)** connects factories holding leftover ("deadstock") fabric with buyers, redirecting material that would otherwise become waste. [Source](../sources/sea-kloth-cares-circular-fashion.md)
- **Fashion Revolution**, the global transparency campaign, has coordinators in **nine Southeast Asian countries**. Its Malaysian coordinator, Sasibai Kimis, said public consciousness of ethical fashion **"is still in its infancy in Malaysia,"** while also noting **"greater consumer awareness on the issues of social justice [in fashion]"** based on media coverage. [Source](../sources/sea-kloth-cares-circular-fashion.md)

## The comparison worth making: Korea's food-waste system

Korea's food-waste system is a *different waste stream* (food, not textiles) run by a *different country* (Korea, not a Southeast Asian one), so it is not directly comparable to the programs above — but it's the one example in this wiki of a household-waste problem being solved at national scale, which is useful as a benchmark of what "solved" looks like:

- **South Korea recycles 95% of its food waste**, under a system where residents **pay by weight**, rolled out in stages: a volume-based fee in **1995** (a **23% reduction in domestic waste** followed), a landfill ban in **2005**, a pilot program in **2010**, and national rollout in **2013**. Household cost: **about $6 a month**. [Source](../sources/korea-food-waste-system.md)
- Notably, **Korea has no equivalent system for its own textile waste** — 800,000 tonnes/year, only 12% recycled locally, 80% handled by unregulated private exporters. [Source](../sources/korea-fast-fashion-boom.md) See [Developed-country consumption and export patterns](developed-country-drivers.md).

The gap this leaves: every Southeast Asian program in this wiki is a civil-society or private initiative (an NGO, a startup, a rental company); none of them is the mandatory, government-run, fee-based system that Korea used to solve its food-waste problem. Indonesia's response to imported clothing, meanwhile, is enforcement (bans and destruction) rather than a collection-and-processing system like Kloth Cares or Korea's food-waste bins. See [Southeast Asia: where developed-country fashion waste lands](southeast-asia-impacts.md).
