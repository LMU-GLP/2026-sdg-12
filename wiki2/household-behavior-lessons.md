# Household Behavior Lessons

What made one household-waste system work, and one fail, and what's missing for fast fashion.

## Korea's food waste: what worked

- Korea recycles **95% of its food waste**. The mechanism is a price signal a household can literally see: RFID-metered bins in Seoul that weigh what a resident throws away and charge them for it, at a household cost of **about $6 a month**. [Source](../sources/korea-food-waste-system.md)
- It took nearly two decades to get there: a volume-based fee in **1995** (which alone produced a **23% reduction in domestic waste**), a landfill ban in **2005**, a pilot in 144 regions in **2010**, and national mandatory rollout in **2013**. [Source](../sources/korea-food-waste-system.md)
- The rollout was staged deliberately: *"The Korean government prioritized reducing food waste by creating the infrastructure needed to manage it, taking time to study barriers to implementation, liaising with restaurant owners, educating the public before the rollout, and eventually enforcing noncompliance."* Public trust grew because residents experienced the system working before enforcement arrived. [Source](../sources/korea-food-waste-system.md)

## California's organic waste: what failed, and it isn't infrastructure

- California's SB 1383 set the country's most ambitious organic-waste diversion targets: **50% below 2014 levels by 2020, and 75% by 2025**. **75% of California communities** report collecting residential organic waste to CalRecycle — the collection infrastructure largely exists. [Source](../sources/la-sb1383-organic-waste.md)
- It's failing anyway: **over 300,000 more tons of food waste went to California landfills in 2022 than in the year SB 1383 was enacted**, per ReFED's tracker. *"At this point no one expects the 2025 target to be met."* [Source](../sources/la-sb1383-organic-waste.md)
- The article is blunt about why: *"It is an open question of how quickly governments and their partners can help them connect this threat to what goes in their trash bins, and convince them to change their habits."* Households, not infrastructure, are the unsolved part. [Source](../sources/la-sb1383-organic-waste.md)

## Applying this to fast fashion

No source in this wiki describes a textile system with Korea's food-waste combination: mandatory participation, a visible per-use price signal, and a staged rollout that built public trust before enforcing. What exists instead, for textiles:

- Korea itself has **no government system** for its own 800,000 tonnes/year of textile waste — 80% goes through unregulated private exporters. [Source](../sources/korea-fast-fashion-boom.md)
- California's textile law (SB 707) is regulatory (extended producer responsibility) but doesn't touch households until its **2030** implementation deadline, and even then the obligation falls on producers, not the price-per-throw model that changed Korean food-waste behavior. [Source](../sources/la-sb707-textile-recovery.md)
- Indonesia's response to imported secondhand clothing is enforcement against sellers (bans, destroyed bundles), not a household-facing collection or fee system. [Source](../sources/indonesia-secondhand-clothing-crackdown.md)
- Southeast Asia's existing programs — Kloth Cares, Style Theory, moreloop — are voluntary and civil-society- or market-driven, not mandatory or government-run. [Source](../sources/sea-kloth-cares-circular-fashion.md)

The open question this wiki leaves for a problem statement: nobody has yet built the textile equivalent of Korea's metered food-waste bin — a household-visible cost or consequence tied to what a household throws away, staged in so that people trust it before it's enforced.
