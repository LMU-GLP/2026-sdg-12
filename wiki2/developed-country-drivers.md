# Developed-Country Consumption and Export Patterns

This page covers the demand side: what households in wealthier countries buy, discard, and export, which sets up the next page on where that waste lands. Three developed/high-income sources are behind this page — South Korea, California/EU-adjacent US, and the EU itself — and they don't all measure the same thing, so their figures are kept separate rather than combined.

## South Korea: a fast-fashion boom with no textile equivalent of its food-waste system

- South Koreans spent **US$58 billion** on fashion products between March 2024 and February 2025.
- South Korea discards an estimated **800,000 tonnes of clothing and textiles annually**, and only **12%** of that is recycled locally.
- **80% of clothing-waste management is handled by the private sector**, not government infrastructure — used clothing exported from Korea goes primarily to **India, Malaysia, and Pakistan**.
- Korea's second-hand clothing market itself is projected to reach **US$30 billion** this year, having **doubled compared to 2021**.
- *"The system we currently have is a problem... [it is] not regulated by the government."* — Juyeon Jung, founder of Wear Again Lab.
[Source](../sources/korea-fast-fashion-boom.md)

This is a direct contrast with [Korea's food-waste system](household-behavior-lessons.md), which is government-run, mandatory, and recycles 95% of food waste. For textiles, Jung's account is that no equivalent government system exists — private exporters absorb the country's used clothing instead.

## California: a 2021 disposal figure and a slow-moving law

- Approximately **1.2 million tons of textiles were disposed of in California in 2021**, according to the office of state Senator Josh Newman, despite **95% of that material being reusable or recyclable**.
- California's SB 707, signed the week of **October 3, 2024**, is described as **"the nation's first extended producer responsibility program for textiles."**
- Its own implementation timeline is slow: PRO approval deadline **March 1, 2026**; program implementation begins **July 1, 2028**; the collection/repair/recycling plan isn't due until **July 1, 2030** — six years after signing.
[Source](../sources/la-sb707-textile-recovery.md)

Nothing about how a California household disposes of old clothes changes under this law before 2030.

## The EU: exports have more than doubled since 2005

- EU countries exported **over 1.44 million tonnes of used textiles in 2025** — more than double the 2005 volume, or about **3.2 kg per person**.
- Export volumes have been **"relatively stable since 2015"** apart from a 2020 dip attributed to Covid-19.
- **48% of 2025 exports went to Asia, 43% to Africa**; Pakistan and the UAE received **"by far the most"** of any single countries.
- The EEA's own account of the outcome is mixed: in Asia, exported clothing is **"mostly recycled or re-exported"**; in both Asia and Africa, some portion **"end up in dumps or burned in the open."**
[Source](../sources/eu-used-textile-exports.md)

## Who exports the most

- By UN Comtrade data, the largest net exporters of secondhand clothing (2019) are **the United States, followed by Germany, the United Kingdom and China**.
- Secondhand clothing is **5% of clothing's traded weight but only 0.5% of its traded value** — a large physical volume moving for comparatively little money.
- Of what's donated in the exporting country, **"usually no more than 10%, is sold domestically"** — the rest is exported or routed to recyclers.
[Source](../sources/global-secondhand-clothing-trade-data.md)

## Reading these together, carefully

These four sources describe different things — Korea's domestic waste stream, a California disposal snapshot, EU export volume, and global exporter rankings — measured by different organizations in different years. None of them should be added together into one "developed-world total." What they share is a pattern: high domestic consumption, low domestic recycling, and a growing volume shipped elsewhere once it's judged unsellable at home.
