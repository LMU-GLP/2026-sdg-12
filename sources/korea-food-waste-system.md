# South Korea's Food Waste System is a Model for Developed Nations

- **Source:** Sarah Marshall, Korea Economic Institute of America (KEIA), July 19, 2022
- **URL:** https://keia.org/analysis/south-koreas-food-waste-system-is-a-model-for-developed-nations/
- **Read on:** 2026-07-30

## Key figures

- **South Korea recycles 95% of its food waste.**
- Koreans generate an average of **130 kg of food waste per person per year**, driven partly by *banchan*, the many small side dishes served with a Korean meal.
- The **volume-based waste fee system, introduced in 1995**, made producers pay the cost of disposal — and led to a **23% reduction in domestic waste.**
- **2005:** Korea outlawed sending food waste to landfills.
- **2010:** pilot program in 144 local regions holding residents financially accountable for food waste.
- **2013:** national rollout. Every resident must dispose of food waste properly and **pay for it by weight.** Fees imposed for noncompliance.
- Cost to a household: **about $6 a month.**
- Processing: roughly **80% of collected food waste is liquid**, turned into biogas. Solids become compost and livestock feed.
- Globally, 1.3 billion tons of food is wasted per year; biodegrading food waste is **6% of global greenhouse gas emissions.**

## How the system works day to day

> "residents in urban areas such as Seoul can take their food waste down to a bin
> that opens by RFID chip. The person scans their chip, the lid opens, they put
> in their food waste, it is weighed, and the resident is charged based on the
> weight."

Outside those areas, residents buy biodegradable bags and leave them in a
designated bin. Dedicated food waste trucks collect weekly.

## Why it worked — the part worth stealing

> "The Korean government prioritized reducing food waste by creating the
> infrastructure needed to manage it, taking time to study barriers to
> implementation, liaising with restaurant owners, educating the public before
> the rollout, and eventually enforcing noncompliance."

The rollout was staged over nearly two decades: fee system 1995, landfill ban
2005, pilot 2010, national system 2013. Public trust grew *because* residents
experienced the system working.

## Note on figures you may see elsewhere

Claims of "6,000 RFID bins in Seoul" and "96.1 billion won per year in economic
benefit" circulate widely but are **not** in this article and we could not verify
them. Don't cite them without a primary source.

## Summary

South Korea recycles 95% of its food waste under a national system where every
household pays by the gram, built up over almost twenty years of staged rollout
from fee systems to a landfill ban to RFID-metered bins. It is a direct answer to
the problem California is currently failing to solve, and the mechanism is
household behavior change driven by a price signal people can see.
