# Problem Statement

## The one-sentence version

Fast fashion bought and discarded by households in South Korea and California/the US ends up as waste that lands on three distinct, named populations in Southeast Asia — Indonesian secondhand-clothing sellers, Cambodian garment-factory workers, and Malaysian consumers — and no country in our research has a mandatory, household-facing system for textiles like the one that already works for food waste.

## The chain we're committing to

**Source (behavior we want to change):** households in **South Korea** and **California/the US**.
**Consequence (who bears the cost):** three separate populations in **Southeast Asia** — Indonesia, Cambodia, and Malaysia — kept separate because they're harmed in three different ways, by three different mechanisms, and none of our sources lets us add their numbers together.

This is a deliberate choice: the "household" whose buying/using/throwing-away habits this project targets is in the developed world. Southeast Asia is where we show the consequence of not changing those habits, not where the intervention lands.

## Why South Korea and California

- South Koreans spent **US$58 billion** on fashion products between March 2024 and February 2025. South Korea discards an estimated **800,000 tonnes of clothing and textiles annually**, and only **12%** of that is recycled locally — **80% of clothing-waste management is handled by the private sector**, not government infrastructure. Used clothing exported from Korea goes primarily to **India, Malaysia, and Pakistan** — a direct, named link from our "source" country to one of our "consequence" countries. Korea's own secondhand-clothing market is separately projected to reach **US$30 billion** this year, having **doubled compared to 2021**. *"The system we currently have is a problem... [it is] not regulated by the government"* — Juyeon Jung, founder of Wear Again Lab. [Source](../sources/korea-fast-fashion-boom.md)
- In California, **approximately 1.2 million tons of textiles were disposed of in 2021**, despite **95% of that material being reusable or recyclable**. California's SB 707, signed the week of October 3, 2024, is **"the nation's first extended producer responsibility program for textiles"** — but its own timeline doesn't require anything of households: PRO approval isn't due until March 1, 2026, program implementation begins July 1, 2028, and the collection/recycling plan isn't due until **July 1, 2030**. [Source](../sources/la-sb707-textile-recovery.md)
- These two figures — Korea's 800,000 tonnes/year and California's 1.2 million tons in a single year — are not the same measurement (a national annual flow vs. a state-level one-year snapshot) and are kept separate, not summed.

## The three Southeast Asian populations, kept separate

**Indonesian secondhand-clothing sellers.** Indonesia has banned secondhand clothing imports since 2015 to protect its domestic textile industry. In a single raid in late March 2023, officials in West Java destroyed **more than 7,000 bundles** of illegally imported secondhand clothing, valued at **$5.3 billion**. For sellers like Siti, this is livelihood, not waste: *"If there are no clothes, that means no money."* [Source](../sources/indonesia-secondhand-clothing-crackdown.md)

**Cambodian garment-factory workers.** Cambodia's garment industry generates **"no less than 90,000 tons of garment waste per year."** Factory offcuts and rejects made for **Nike, Ralph Lauren, Diesel, and Clarks** are burned in Cambodian brick kilns operating around the clock at temperatures **"up to 650C."** Because most garments are polyester, burning them **"exposes bonded workers to toxic fumes and microplastic fibres"**; these workers are described as engaged in **"a form of modern-day slavery."** [Source](../sources/cambodia-fast-fashion-waste-burning.md) (Note: this is production-side waste from garments made for global brands generally — our source does not tie this specific waste stream to Korean or Californian consumption by name, the way it does for Korea's clothing exports to Malaysia above.)

**Malaysian consumers/households.** Malaysia imports an estimated **220,000 tonnes/year** of secondhand clothing — the only Southeast Asian country in the world's top four importers by weight, behind Pakistan (640,000 tonnes/year) and India (330,000 tonnes/year), and roughly on par with Angola (190,000 tonnes/year). [Source](../sources/global-secondhand-clothing-trade-data.md) Malaysia is separately estimated to generate its own **~2,000 tonnes of textile waste a day**. [Source](../sources/sea-kloth-cares-circular-fashion.md) This is the population our chain names directly, since Korea's used clothing is exported "primarily to India, Malaysia, and Pakistan." [Source](../sources/korea-fast-fashion-boom.md)

## What our sources don't give us — said plainly, not filled in

- **No headcounts.** We do not have a count of how many Indonesian sellers, Cambodian workers, or Malaysian households are affected — only tonnage and dollar figures. We chose not to substitute Indonesia's "26 million people below the poverty line" figure for a seller headcount, because that figure is general poverty context in our source, not a count of people in the secondhand-clothing trade specifically. [Source](../sources/indonesia-secondhand-clothing-crackdown.md)
- **No Southeast Asia-specific UN target.** Goal 12 has a specific number for food waste (Target 12.3: halve it by 2030) but no textile-specific target — a fast-fashion project sits under the general "waste generation" language of Target 12.5. [Source](../sources/un-sdg12-targets.md)
- **No delivery program at the international level.** The UN Alliance for Sustainable Fashion, launched in 2019 by eight UN and partner agencies, coordinates policy and analysis — its own stated method is to find "gaps" in UN action and push that analysis into policy, not to fund or run collection/recycling programs on the ground. [Source](../sources/un-alliance-sustainable-fashion.md)
- **No proof that Cambodia's factory waste is Korean- or Californian-sourced specifically.** Our source ties the Cambodia brick-kiln story to Nike, Ralph Lauren, Diesel, and Clarks — global brands, not a Korea/California-specific supply chain. Treat this as evidence that "fast fashion for developed-country markets generally" causes this harm, not proof of a direct Korea-to-Cambodia or California-to-Cambodia link.

## The benchmark for what "solved" looks like

South Korea's own food-waste system is the one household-waste problem in our research that's actually been solved at national scale: **95% of food waste recycled**, under a system where households **pay by weight**, rolled out in stages from a 1995 fee to a 2013 mandatory national system. [Source](../sources/korea-food-waste-system.md) Notably, Korea has built no equivalent system for its own textile waste — the same country solved one household-waste stream and left the other to unregulated private exporters. That gap, inside one country, is the strongest evidence in our research that the barrier isn't technical.
