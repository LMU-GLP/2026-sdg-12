# Southeast Asia: fast fashion (Indonesia case)

Sources: [Indonesia secondhand clothing
crackdown](../sources/indonesia-secondhand-clothing-crackdown.md),
[CASHEW program](../sources/cashew-indonesia-fast-fashion-program.md)

Our two Southeast Asia-specific sources both come from Indonesia. Neither is
a nationwide waste-tonnage dataset — one is a policy/enforcement story and
one is a youth education program's own survey data. This page keeps that
distinction visible rather than treating either as a national statistic.

## The problem, as our source frames it: secondhand clothing as both supply and threat

Indonesia has **banned secondhand clothing imports since 2015**, citing
public health and domestic textile industry protection. In **late March
2023**, trade minister Zulkifli Hasan oversaw the destruction of illegally
imported used clothes across **more than 7,000 bundles** in West Java —
roughly **80 billion rupiah (about $5.3 million)** in market value, per
cross-referenced Indonesian reporting
([Indonesia secondhand clothing crackdown](../sources/indonesia-secondhand-clothing-crackdown.md)).

The stated rationale mixes health, environmental, and economic protection
arguments: *"used clothing presents a risk as it could carry bacteria or
fungi,"* per Rio Priambodo of the Indonesian Consumer Foundation, who also
raised the concern that low-quality secondhand items become landfill waste
([Indonesia secondhand clothing crackdown](../sources/indonesia-secondhand-clothing-crackdown.md)).

## The household side of the story

**Over 26 million Indonesians live officially below the poverty line**,
which the source cites as the reason secondhand clothing — sourced from
Korea, Japan, and Singapore — has real household demand. A Medan vendor
selling secondhand stock told the source: *"If there are no clothes, that
means no money."*
([Indonesia secondhand clothing crackdown](../sources/indonesia-secondhand-clothing-crackdown.md))

This is worth flagging against [SDG Target 12.5](fast-fashion-sdg12.md),
which names *reuse* as a goal alongside prevention, reduction, and
recycling: Indonesia's enforcement response is aimed at the *import* channel
of reuse, not at reducing new fast-fashion consumption, and it's cutting
against a lower-income household's cheapest option rather than a fast-
fashion retailer's supply chain.

## The awareness side of the story

Separately, a 2023 youth program (CASHEW, run by CIMSA-Indonesia) surveyed
its own target audience and found **"more than 50% didn't know about fast
fashion and fashion waste"** and **"70% of respondents do not know how to
overcome it"**
([CASHEW program](../sources/cashew-indonesia-fast-fashion-program.md)).
See [Fast fashion solutions: CASHEW](fast-fashion-solutions.md) for the
program itself.

## A note on an uncorroborated figure

The CASHEW source states Indonesia ranks **second globally** in textile
waste contribution. Our global dataset source
([Global fast fashion waste stats](../sources/global-fast-fashion-waste-stats.md))
has no country ranking to check this against — China and the US are the
only countries it ranks, and Indonesia isn't among them. This claim is kept
here, attributed to CASHEW specifically, rather than stated as an
independently verified fact.

## What's missing

Neither source gives a per-capita or per-household textile-waste generation
figure for Indonesia or Southeast Asia more broadly — the kind of number
the [South Korea food-waste source](../sources/korea-food-waste-system.md)
had for food (130 kg/person/year). If the team wants that comparison, that
source doesn't exist in this wiki yet.
