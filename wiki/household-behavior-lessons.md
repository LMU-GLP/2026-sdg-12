# What food waste policy teaches about changing household behavior

The two sources this wiki started from are about food waste, not fashion —
but they're the only evidence here with a multi-year track record of actually
changing what households throw away, so the mechanism is worth extracting
even though the waste stream is different.

## What worked, in Korea

Korea's food-waste system took **almost twenty years to build**: a disposal
fee in 1995, a landfill ban in 2005, a 144-region pilot in 2010, and national,
mandatory, weight-based enforcement only from 2013
[Korea food waste system](../sources/korea-food-waste-system.md). The
result — 95% of food waste recycled — came from *residents paying by the
gram*, a visible price signal, not from an awareness campaign
[Korea food waste system](../sources/korea-food-waste-system.md). The
Korea Economic Institute's account credits the staged rollout itself: the
government "creat[ed] the infrastructure needed to manage it, tak[ing] time
to study barriers to implementation, liaising with restaurant owners,
educating the public before the rollout, and eventually enforcing
noncompliance"
[Korea food waste system](../sources/korea-food-waste-system.md).

## What failed, in California

SB 1383 set a household-facing organics target and largely built the
collection infrastructure — 75% of California communities report collecting
residential organic waste — but diversion still went the wrong direction:
**over 300,000 more tons of food waste went to landfills in 2022 than the
year the law passed**
[California SB 1383](../sources/la-sb1383-organic-waste.md). The reporting
is direct about why: infrastructure existed and behavior didn't follow it.
"It is an open question of how quickly governments and their partners can
help [households] connect this threat to what goes in their trash bins, and
convince them to change their habits"
[California SB 1383](../sources/la-sb1383-organic-waste.md).

## Applied to fast fashion

Every fashion program in this wiki looks structurally more like California's
starting point than Korea's finished one: infrastructure or a law exists (or
is pending), but nothing yet creates the kind of visible, individual price
signal that Korea's RFID bins gave households for food.

- SB 707 mandates producer-side collection infrastructure — the same
  category of move California made for organic waste with SB 1383 — but
  attaches no fee or signal a household would feel
  [California SB 707](../sources/la-sb707-textile-recovery.md).
- Korea itself has *not* applied its own food-waste playbook to textiles: 80%
  of clothing waste handling is private-sector, with no fee system, per
  Wear Again Lab's founder
  [Korea fast fashion boom](../sources/korea-fast-fashion-boom.md).
- The Southeast Asian programs in this wiki (Kloth Cares, Style Theory,
  moreloop) are closer to *awareness and access* interventions — nearer to
  California's "K-12 curriculum" response to organic waste
  [California SB 1383](../sources/la-sb1383-organic-waste.md) — than to a
  price-based system like Korea's.
