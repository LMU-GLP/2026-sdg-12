# Reloop: solution concept

Reloop is a website that collects unwanted clothing from US fast-fashion
shoppers, uses an AI system to sort it into reusable vs. damaged, donates the
reusable share domestically, and turns the damaged share into new products
(tote bags, socks, picnic blankets, T-shirts, seasonal gift items) at an
in-house manufacturing facility. Contributors earn "Loops" points redeemable
for benefits and discounts. This page records the team's answers from a
structured interview about the concept — worked out one decision at a time —
plus the places where the concept still creates tension with the [problem
statement](../CLAUDE.md) it's meant to answer.

## Who it's for

- **Geography: United States, launched nationally** (not California-only,
  not Korea). The team considered a California-only launch — which would
  have matched [SB 707's household-mandate gap](../sources/la-sb707-textile-recovery.md)
  directly (nothing required of households until 2028–2030) — and a global
  launch, but chose national US instead.
- **User: fast-fashion shoppers specifically**, not general households doing
  a closet cleanout. This is who the SHEIN pop-up plan targets.
- **Korea is explicitly out of scope for Reloop.** The team dropped it from
  the solution after the first interview question. This means Reloop does
  not address the [South Korea](south-korea.md) side of the problem
  statement's chain at all — the $58B Korean fast-fashion market, the
  800,000 tonnes/year discarded there, and Wear Again Lab's push for
  regulation remain undiverted by this project.

## What it actually does

1. Customer sends clothes to Reloop (see collection, below).
2. An AI scanning system checks condition and recyclability.
3. Reusable items are **donated domestically only** — not exported. The team
   confirmed this explicitly, to avoid Reloop becoming another link in the
   used-clothing export chain the problem statement documents (Korea's
   exports "primarily to India, Malaysia, and Pakistan"; Malaysia's ~220,000
   tonnes/year of imports — see [Southeast Asia](southeast-asia.md)).
4. Damaged items are separated by material and manufactured in-house into
   tote bags, socks, picnic blankets, T-shirts, and seasonal gift items.
5. Contributors earn Loops points, scaled to weight and condition, redeemable
   for benefits and discounts.
6. Revenue comes from five channels: selling the recycled products, paid
   memberships, investment, donations, and partnerships with brands Reloop
   judges "environmentally responsible." Approved partner brands get a badge
   based on standards such as recyclable materials and natural dyes.
7. Every product carries a signature Reloop tag, for brand recognition.

## How it would work in the real world

- **Collection: mail-in and scheduled pickup**, both available — no
  drop-off bins/kiosks.
- **Cost: the customer pays a small flat fee** for shipping/pickup (not
  free, not free-above-a-threshold), in exchange for Loops points.
- **Manufacturing: in-house.** Reloop builds and runs its own facility to
  turn sorted material into finished products, rather than partnering with
  existing upcyclers or wholesaling the sorted material. This is the
  heaviest capital commitment in the plan and the team should treat it as
  such — it's a manufacturing buildout, not just a logistics/software
  product.
- **Promotion: paid pop-up ads on fast-fashion sites like SHEIN**, pursued
  first as a straightforward paid placement and second as a formal
  partnership if SHEIN agrees to host it; **social media advertising as the
  fallback** if SHEIN declines both. The team should note the tension here
  even though they're proceeding: SHEIN is the kind of retailer whose sales
  volume feeds the waste stream this project responds to, so advertising
  Reloop *on* SHEIN is a direct engagement with the source of the problem,
  not a neutral ad buy.

## What's deliberately left out

- **No direct program for the three Southeast Asian populations** named in
  the problem statement — Indonesian secondhand-clothing sellers, Cambodian
  garment-factory workers, Malaysian consumers/households. This is
  consistent with the problem statement's own framing: the household is
  the intervention point, Southeast Asia is where the consequence is shown,
  not where the intervention lands. Reloop does not claim to help, compensate,
  or change conditions for any of these three populations directly.
- **No policy or lobbying work.** Reloop is a consumer product/business, not
  an advocacy organization — it does not push for a federal EPR law or try
  to accelerate SB 707's timeline.
- **No Korea-side intervention** (see above) — a gap worth naming since half
  of the problem statement's "source" side (Korea) now has no corresponding
  solution in this wiki.

## Open tensions the team should keep tracking

- Going national instead of California-only forfeits the SB 707
  regulatory-gap argument — outside California there is no equivalent law
  whose timeline Reloop is filling ahead of. The justification for "why now"
  shifts from *closing a specific legislated gap* to a broader, unsourced
  claim that no textile recycling infrastructure exists anywhere in the US.
- Advertising on SHEIN is a deliberate, if unresolved, contradiction: using
  the fast-fashion retailer's own audience and platform to fund and promote
  the response to fast-fashion waste.
- In-house manufacturing is the most operationally demanding piece of the
  plan and hasn't been stress-tested against the alternative (partnering
  with existing upcyclers, as [Kloth Cares](existing-solutions.md) and
  moreloop already do in Southeast Asia).
